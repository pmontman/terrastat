An open dataset · in progress

A dataset of the world’s economic time series.

1.05 B time series of official economic statistics — prices, trade, output, labour, migration, health, energy — collected from FRED, Eurostat and the OECD, and put on one uniform schema so you can train and evaluate models across all of them at once. Every series arrives with its own licence and the credit its publisher asks for, so cutting a subset you are allowed to share is a one-line filter.

Three sources so far, and growing. The schema is built to absorb more, and more are planned — energy, trade and web-activity series among them.

Getting started

Two ways in, depending on whether you want the data or the crawl.

I want to train something

Load a snapshot, split it without leaking, cut supervised windows. Runs the same against a 28 MB starter set or the full corpus.

# pip install terrastat
from terrastat import dataset

lf = dataset.load("starter")
train, test = dataset.split(lf, by="dataset_id")
X, y = dataset.windows(train, horizon=18)

The payload is plain Parquet plus a checksummed manifest — readable with polars, pandas, DuckDB or Hugging Face datasets, with nothing of ours installed. The public snapshot is still being prepared; until it lands, terrastat export starter --public-only builds one from your own crawl.

I want to gather it myself

One command, re-run until it says nothing is left. Add a FRED key to .env first; Eurostat and the OECD need none.

pip install -e .
terrastat run # all sources, resumable
terrastat run --max-hours 8 # stop cleanly, resume later

terrastat corpus # regenerate this page
terrastat quality --out reports/quality
terrastat search "chicken|poultry"

The manual walks through opening a terminal, stopping and resuming, and reading what lands on disk.

The schema

One row per series, 36 columns.

The sources publish in formats that have nothing in common. They are mapped onto the same 36 columns, so the whole corpus opens as one table whichever source a series came from. The columns fall into seven groups.

identity4

The key for a series, and the table it came from. The dataset id is what you group on when splitting.

series_uidsourcesource_iddataset_id

human-readable4

A description per series, built from all the metadata the publisher gave. You can feed it to a text encoder alongside the values, or ignore it.

dataset_titletitledescriptionnotes

classification8

Where the series sits in the published table. Each dimension keeps its code and its label, so totals, their parts and nested geographies (DE, DE1, DE11) can be found.

frequencyfrequency_rawunitsseasonal_adjustmentgeogeo_labeldimensionstags

the data3

Dates, values and status flags, stored as lists on the row. Missing observations stay as nulls rather than disappearing, and flags such as provisional or estimated are kept.

datesvaluesflags

licence6

The licence, its URL, and the credit the publisher asks for. Filtering on these gives a subset you can share.

license_idlicense_namelicense_urllicense_detailattributionlicense_notes

extent5

Start, end and counts. They are fixed-width, so a scan that reads only these never touches the values: a pass over the whole corpus takes about twelve seconds.

start_dateend_daten_pointsn_obsn_flagged

provenance6

Who produced the number, when it was fetched, and which vintage it is. FRED republishes 80,274 OECD series, so the originating agency is worth keeping.

origin_agenciesorigin_us_federalsource_urllast_updatedretrieved_atvintage

Four rows from the corpus, with different sources, frequencies and subjects. Each is drawn against its own range, so the heights are not comparable.

Curation and leakage

This kind of data is prone to leakage.

Series are kept as published, with their own start, end and gaps. The part worth knowing about is that economic statistics come with pitfalls that are easy to miss, and most of them make a random train/test split look better than it is. Twelve are written up so far, and we keep finding more.

The same series in another form

Seasonally adjusted next to raw, an index next to its level, another currency, a per-capita version. Different numbers, same information.

Accounting identities

Geographic and product hierarchies, national accounts identities, mirror trade, stock and flow pairs. A total is a sum of series that can end up on the other side of the split.

Revisions

Only the latest vintage is stored. A 2008 value is the number as it reads now, not as it was published then. A backtest that is not real-time uses data that was not available at the time.

Publication lag and projections

A quarterly figure is released weeks after the period it covers. Some series run to 2100 because they are forecasts. Both look like history.

Filters and imputation

Smoothed, interpolated and estimated values carry information backwards inside a single series. Splitting cannot fix that; the status flags are there to find it.

Ill-formed series

Constants, series that are still updated but have not moved in years, series that are almost entirely one value. They pass any length or recency filter and teach a model nothing. The sampled table below counts them.

The same series from two sources

FRED republishes 80,274 OECD series, and there are near-duplicates within single sources too. origin_agencies is there to find them.

This is why the split groups by dataset instead of shuffling rows, and why the cleaning routine is opt-in rather than the default.

A long series that barely moves is common here, and close to useless as a training example. What is in it

What is in it.

Broad, and deliberately uncurated

Daily to five-yearly, national accounts to fisheries by species, with histories reaching back to the seventeenth century and forward to published projections. Series keep their own start, end and gaps: what counts as clean depends on the question, so nothing is trimmed on your behalf.

Licences are sorted out

Terms differ by agency and, for FRED, by series. Every row carries its own license_id, license_url, attribution and originating agency, so selecting a subset you can redistribute is a filter, and the export writes out the citations each source asks for.

One row per series

The same 36 columns whatever the source, so the whole thing opens as one dataset in polars, pandas or DuckDB. Dates, values and status flags sit as lists on the row, next to the metadata.

Made for leakage-aware splits

The sources republish one another, totals sit next to their parts, and the same indicator turns up at several frequencies. Twelve of these are documented, and the splitting that ships groups by dataset instead of shuffling rows.

Before you redistribute anything

The code is Apache-2.0. The data is not.

Licences travel with the rows, and they differ

Eurostat and the OECD permit re-use, including commercial re-use, with attribution. FRED reports a copyright status per series, and its own terms restrict archiving and machine-learning use of material that passes through it — while the underlying US federal data is public domain at its origin.

The subset to publish is therefore a filter, not a whole corpus: terrastat export NAME --public-only keeps Eurostat, the OECD, and the FRED rows that are both public domain and federal in origin, and writes an ATTRIBUTIONS.md beside the data. Check license_id and attribution on the rows you actually use.
